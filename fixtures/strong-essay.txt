Unpaid community service is often proposed as a compulsory element of secondary education. I largely agree that schools should require it, provided the placements are well designed and do not displace academic study.

Compulsory service can develop a sense of responsibility that classrooms rarely teach on their own. When teenagers spend time in a library, a shelter, or a local park, they practise punctuality, teamwork, and basic problem-solving. For example, a student who helps older residents with grocery shopping has to plan a route, listen carefully, and adapt when something unexpected happens. These are practical habits, and they also make abstract civic ideas feel concrete. Over a school year, that repeated contact can soften the idea that civic duties belong only to adults.

There are, however, legitimate objections. Some families already rely on teenagers for paid work or childcare, so an extra obligation can create unfair pressure. In addition, poorly organised schemes waste time and breed resentment. Schools should therefore keep the requirement modest — perhaps two hours a week — and offer a range of placements, including options that can be completed at weekends. Flexibility would protect students who face genuine constraints while preserving the educational aim. Clear guidance from teachers matters as much as the hours themselves, because students need to understand who benefits and how their effort will be assessed.

In conclusion, mandatory community service is justified when it is limited, varied, and supervised. It will not transform every adolescent, but it can broaden their experience of society and encourage them to contribute before they leave school. Used in this way, the policy supports both the student and the community.
